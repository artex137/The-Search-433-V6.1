Could we send a spaceship to Comet 3I/ATLAS to collect samples and bring them back for analysis?

Experts have raised the question of whether a spacecraft could be sent to Comet 3I/ATLAS to collect samples of its gas, dust, and surface for analysis. The discussion comes in light of recent advancements in space exploration, particularly regarding interstellar objects. Notably, Comet 3I/ATLAS passed near Earth in 2023.

This inquiry is significant as it could provide insights into the composition and formation of comets, similar to what was learned from previous missions like the European Space Agency’s Rosetta and NASA’s Stardust. However, experts note that the challenges of reaching and returning from such a distant object are substantial.

Key facts

  • Comet 3I/ATLAS passed near Earth in 2023.
  • ESA’s Rosetta mission took 10 years to reach Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko.
  • NASA’s Stardust collected samples from Comet 81P/Wild and returned them to Earth.
  • Both Rosetta and Stardust missions took extensive planning and were aimed at well-known comets.
  • Project Lyra proposes using a gravitational slingshot maneuver to reach interstellar objects.
  • Comet Interceptor is set to launch in 2028 or 2029 to study new comets.
  • 3I/ATLAS did not pass close enough to Earth for Comet Interceptor to reach.

Timeline

In 2023, Comet 3I/ATLAS made a close approach to Earth. This event has prompted discussions about the feasibility of sending a spacecraft to collect samples. Previous missions, such as ESA's Rosetta and NASA's Stardust, have provided valuable data on other comets but have faced numerous challenges.

The Comet Interceptor mission, planned for launch in 2028 or 2029, aims to address some of these challenges by remaining in a "parking orbit" to wait for new comets. However, it will not be able to reach Comet 3I/ATLAS due to its trajectory.

Official statements

“It would be great to get a spacecraft to an interstellar object to see it up close and to sample what it is made of,” said Raymond Tanguay, an expert in planetary science.
“The challenge of visiting a newly discovered comet is that we don’t have enough warning time to design and build a spacecraft,” added Colin Snodgrass, Professor of Planetary Astronomy at the University of Edinburgh.

Context

Previous missions to comets have significantly advanced our understanding of these celestial bodies. The Rosetta mission, for instance, provided detailed information about Comet 67P, while Stardust returned samples that have been crucial for research. However, the complexity of reaching newly discovered comets, like 3I/ATLAS, presents new challenges, particularly in terms of trajectory and timing.

What’s next

As space agencies continue to explore options for studying comets, the focus will remain on developing missions that can adapt to newly discovered objects. The success of the Comet Interceptor mission may pave the way for future explorations of interstellar comets in the 2030s.
