A comet from another star system arrived in our solar system in July 2025 carrying water ice from a planetary system that formed billions of years before our Sun — the oldest water ever observed passing through our neighbourhood

Published 2026-08-16 03:05 UTC

Comet 3I/ATLAS carries ancient water ice through our solar system

Interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS, discovered by astronomers on July 1, 2025, has carried water ice that may date back 11 to 12 billion years, according to a study published in the journal Nature in June 2026. The comet's ice is believed to have formed long before the Sun and planets, making it the oldest water ever observed in our solar neighborhood.

This finding is significant as it provides insights into the early chemical evolution of the Milky Way and the conditions present when the solar system formed. NASA and the astronomical community are now using data from the James Webb Space Telescope to better understand the origins and composition of this interstellar object.

Key facts

  • Discovered on July 1, 2025, by the NASA-funded ATLAS survey telescope in Rio Hurtado, Chile.
  • Water ice in 3I/ATLAS may have formed 11 to 12 billion years ago, predating the solar system by 6 to 7 billion years.
  • Measurements were made by the James Webb Space Telescope on December 22 and 23, 2025.
  • The comet is the third confirmed interstellar object, following 1I/‘Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov.
  • The deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio in the water ice was measured at 0.98 percent.
  • Carbon ratios in the comet's coma suggest it formed in a cold and metal-poor environment.

Timeline

On June 14, 2025, pre-discovery images of 3I/ATLAS were captured before its official discovery on July 1. The comet entered the solar system prior to its detection and was already moving inward. It was identified as an interstellar object based on its hyperbolic trajectory, indicating it is not gravitationally bound to the Sun.

On December 22 and 23, 2025, the James Webb Space Telescope conducted observations of the comet, measuring its chemical composition. The results were analyzed and published in a peer-reviewed study in June 2026, suggesting the comet's water ice formed during a period of intense star formation in the early universe.

Official statements

“3I/ATLAS is a fragment of an ancient planetary system,” said Martin Cordiner of NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center, who led the study. “The measured chemistry indicates a cold, old and relatively metal-poor formation environment.”

Context

This discovery adds to the understanding of interstellar comets, which have been observed only a few times. Prior interstellar objects, such as 1I/‘Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov, have provided valuable data, but 3I/ATLAS offers a unique glimpse into the early chemical processes of the Milky Way.

The findings from 3I/ATLAS are particularly important as they challenge assumptions about the composition of materials present in our solar system and how they compare to those found in interstellar space.

What’s next

Researchers aim to observe additional interstellar comets to compare their compositions and further understand the population of ancient icy bodies moving between stars. Future studies may refine the estimates of the ages of these objects and their formation environments.
