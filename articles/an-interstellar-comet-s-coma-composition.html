An Interstellar Comet’s Coma Composition

A group of astronomers has released findings on the composition of the coma of the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS, based on observations made in December 2022. The researchers, led by Nathan Roth from NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, collected high-quality data as the comet traveled away from the Sun.

This research is significant as it provides insights into the chemical makeup of a comet that is not part of our solar system. The findings may help scientists understand the differences between interstellar comets and those from our solar system. The observations were made using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and its Integral Field Unit (IFU) in the Near Infrared Spectrometer.

Key facts

  • Comet 3I/ATLAS was observed in December 2022.
  • Observations were conducted at approximately 2.4 astronomical units (au) from the Sun.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO) was found to be more abundant than water in the comet's coma.
  • Different molecules were found to be outgassing in unique directions from the coma.
  • The research team included astronomers from NASA Goddard Space Flight Center.

Timeline

In December 2022, astronomers observed comet 3I/ATLAS as it was retreating from the Sun. Using JWST’s IFU, they captured detailed images and spectra of the comet's coma. These observations allowed the team to map the spatial distribution of various molecules and their emission lines.

The analysis revealed that CO was the dominant molecule in the coma, contrasting with typical solar system comets, where water is most abundant. The team noted that the distance from the Sun at which these observations were made likely influenced the results.

Official statements

“The observations show how much we can learn in the brief time interstellar visitors spend in our solar system,” said Nathan Roth, the lead researcher from NASA Goddard Space Flight Center.

Context

Interstellar comets like 3I/ATLAS provide a unique opportunity to study materials that have not been altered by solar system processes. Prior to this, similar observations of other interstellar objects have been limited. The findings from 3I/ATLAS may inform future studies of other interstellar comets.

What’s next

As comet 3I/ATLAS has now moved too far and faint for observations, researchers will prepare for the next interstellar comet that enters the solar system. Future studies will likely employ similar observational techniques to gain further insights into these celestial bodies.
